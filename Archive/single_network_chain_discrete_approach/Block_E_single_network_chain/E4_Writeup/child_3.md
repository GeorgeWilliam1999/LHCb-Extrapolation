# Protocol 7: the grid and the farm
Sixteen networks, one farm job each: step counts 2, 64, 128 and 256, which are step lengths of 2,588.9, 80.9, 40.5 and 20.2 mm, by stage counts 2, 4, 8 and 16. They were submitted as cluster 5805460 on 17 September 2026, continued as cluster 5806728, and extended after the stopping lesson below as clusters 5809659, 5815090 and 5816352. A background keeper released memory-held and wall-time-held jobs every half hour and resubmitted anything that left the queue unfinished. In total the sixteen runs took **610 hours** of single-core training wall time, from 4.2 hours for the cheapest to 77.0 hours for the most expensive, over 109 to 1,758 restarts and 6 to 70 rounds. # Protocol 8: stopping, and the lesson
The trainer was written to stop when a refreshed round's first two restarts each gained under 1 per cent. **At 64 steps and above that rule never fired.** With the rescaled loss of protocol 5, every restart kept cutting the loss by more than 1 per cent long after the validation error had stopped moving, and the runs at 64 steps and above ran into their restart cap instead. On 18 September 2026, with the loss on freshly drawn states flat for ten rounds, training was stopped by hand and every network was re-scored from the weights then on disk. Those checkpoints are kept, and every table and figure in the results describes them.
**That stop was premature, and the loss was what misled us.** The loss was flat; the validation error was not. The rule that settles it was written the same evening and applied to these runs alone before any later work existed, so that it could not be tuned to a result. In words: a run has stopped improving when the median validation error of its last ten rounds is no more than 5 per cent below the median of the ten before it, and that holds at each of the last three rounds. As a formula, writing $`v_1 \ldots v_R`$ for the median validation error after each round, with a window $`W = 10`$, a tolerance $`\tau = 0.05`$ and a hold $`H = 3`$:
$$
\text{finished} \iff \operatorname{med}\big(v_{r-W+1 \ldots r}\big) \ \geq\ (1 - \tau)\ \operatorname{med}\big(v_{r-2W+1 \ldots r-W}\big) \quad \text{for } r = R-H+1,\ \ldots,\ R .
$$
The rule is deliberately one-sided. A two-sided test was tried first and thrown away: the round-to-round error wanders by 8 to 23 per cent, so two ten-round medians rarely land within 5 per cent of each other even when a run has long finished. The question is not whether the error is steady, it is whether the error is still getting better. The hold of three rounds is there because a single crossing can happen by chance, and it caught two runs that met the condition once and then resumed falling.
Applied to the state of the runs on the evening they were stopped, **eleven of the eleven runs with twenty or more rounds were still improving, by 5 to 24 per cent per ten rounds**: 64 steps at 2, 4, 8 and 16 stages by 18, 12, 15 and 24 per cent; 128 steps at 2, 4 and 8 stages by 12, 5 and 6 per cent; 256 steps at 2, 4, 8 and 16 stages by 5, 9, 19 and 16 per cent. Eleven falls out of eleven has probability 0.000488 if the wandering were pure noise. All fifteen extendable runs were put back to training; the two-step, two-stage run was left alone, because it already sits at the exact scheme's own error and no amount of training can help it.
The lesson, in one sentence: **with a rescaled loss, convergence cannot be read from the loss.**
The rule also decides when a run stops, through a keeper that stops resubmitting once it holds. So the validation split is used for stopping, not only for monitoring; the test split is untouched by it.
# Protocol 9: the comparators
1. **The exact collocation scheme, chained the same way.** The $`q`$-stage Gauss-Legendre equations solved with a root-finder to a residual of $`10^{-9}`$, starting from the straight line, leg after leg across the magnet with the solved end state of one leg feeding the next, exactly as the networks are chained. Its error against the reference is the scheme's own discretisation error at that step and stage count, and it is the ceiling. New runs were made at 2 and 256 steps (cluster 5805461, every solve converged); the earlier study's runs at 64 and 128 steps are reused, which the track gate of protocol 1 makes legitimate because the test particles are the same.
2. **The straight line.** The start state carried to $`z_1`$ with the magnet switched off: median radial error 450,542 µm on the test tracks, which is the size of the thing being modelled.
3. **The per-step-network chains of the previous study**, at the step and stage counts the two studies share, re-scored radially on the same test tracks from that study's own stored chain states. The caveat carried forward with them is that their training was ended early by the optimiser tolerances that the rescaling of protocol 5 exists to defeat, and that they trained on 2,000 particles rather than 11,567, because a per-step network need only see its own plane.
4. **The material floor.** The particle's real state on its own first fibre-tracker plane against the reference track carried to the same plane: median 1,896 µm radially, 95th percentile 13,432 µm, over the test tracks. It is the difference the material makes, and no field-only method can go below it against the real state. It is used here only as a scale; the companion page named in the introduction is where it is taken apart.
# Protocol 10: the scoring
1. **Which tracks.** Everything reported is on the 1,452 **test** tracks, never used in training and never used to choose anything, except where the text says validation. The validation split does two jobs: it chooses the case-study network, and, through the plateau rule, it decides when a run stops. The test split does neither.
2. **Endpoint error.** The network applied $`N`$ times from the track's real state carried to $`z_0`$, its state on $`z_1`$ compared with the reference track there. Radial, $`\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}`$, unless the text names the **max metric**, $`\max(|\Delta x|, |\Delta y|)`$, which runs about 10 to 20 per cent lower and which earlier tables in this programme used.
3. **Single-step error.** One application of the network from a reference state on a start plane, compared with the reference one step later. This is a different quantity from the endpoint error and is always named as such. It is evaluated on every (track, plane) pair of the test split: 2,904 pairs at 2 steps, 92,928 at 64, 185,856 at 128 and 371,712 at 256.
4. **Along the crossing.** The same radial comparison at every plane of the chain.
5. **The anatomy.** For one network, at every step, the network's output is compared with the reference taken from **the same state the network was given**, so that what is measured is what this step added rather than what it inherited. Three quantities follow. The **local** position and slope error of a step. The **lever arm** model, each step's slope error multiplied by the distance still to travel to $`z_1`$, summed over the chain, which tests whether what matters is the size of a step's error or how early it was made. And the **coherence** of a track's slope increments, $`\big|\sum_k \delta_k\big| / \sum_k |\delta_k|`$, which is 1 when every step pushes the same way and $`1/\sqrt{N}`$, that is 0.125 at 64 steps, when the steps are independent.
6. **The case study.** One network is singled out for detailed treatment. It is chosen on the **validation** split, by the mean validation error over the last eight rounds, so that the test tracks play no part in picking it. That choice is the 64-step, two-stage network, step length 80.9 mm.
