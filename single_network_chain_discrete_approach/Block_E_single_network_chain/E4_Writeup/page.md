# 1. Introduction
## 1.1 The problem
LHCb is a forward spectrometer at the Large Hadron Collider. A charged particle leaves signals in the vertex detector, in the four planes of the Upstream Tracker in front of the magnet, and in the twelve layers of the scintillating-fibre tracker behind it. Between those two detectors it crosses a dipole magnet of about one tesla over five metres and its path bends, by an amount inversely proportional to its momentum. That bend is how the momentum is measured.
Reconstructing a track means fitting a trajectory through those signals. The fit works with a **track state** on a plane of constant $`z`$, the coordinate along the beam: five numbers $`(x, y, t_x, t_y, q/p)`$, where $`x`$ and $`y`$ are transverse positions in millimetres, $`t_x = dx/dz`$ and $`t_y = dy/dz`$ are the two slopes, which are dimensionless, and $`q/p`$ is the signed inverse momentum. Every iteration of the fit carries a state from one plane to another through the field, millions of times per event. The routine that does it is the **track extrapolator**, the most expensive one in the fit across the magnet, because it integrates the equation of motion numerically, reading the measured field map at every step.
The crossing studied here is fixed: from $`z_0 = 2{,}648.2`$ mm, the last Upstream Tracker plane, to $`z_1 = 7{,}826.0`$ mm, the first fibre-tracker plane, a distance $`L = 5{,}177.8`$ mm. On the axis the field rises from about 0.21 T at $`z_0`$ to about 1.05 T near $`z = 4.7`$ m and falls to about 0.30 T at $`z_1`$, so a step of a given length does different work depending on where it starts. That fact drives the design of the network.
## 1.2 What came before, and why this study
The programme asks whether a small neural network can replace that routine, trained **without labels**: not by showing it where particles went, but by requiring that its own outputs satisfy the equations of an implicit Runge-Kutta scheme, the discrete-time construction of Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis (2019, section 3.2).
The study before this one, <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3dc5d544b9d9813fa4a1dfba8da3cc98"/>, trained **a different network for every step**, 128 for a 128-step crossing, each on the previous one's predictions. That follows the paper literally, because the paper's network takes the spatial coordinate as its input, so each network belongs to one step. It does not follow once the input is the *state*: then one network can serve every step. On 16 September 2026 George ruled that this is what he wanted, **one network of step **$`\Delta z`$** applied **$`N`$** times**, which is the construction of Wang and Perdikaris (2021, arXiv 2106.05384).
The work lives in `single_network_chain_discrete_approach/Block_E_single_network_chain/`, the only place in this write-up where the internal block letter appears. Every figure, table and legend names a network by what it is: its step count, its stage count and its step length.
## 1.3 What the networks are measured against
Every number compares a prediction with a **field-only reference track**: the same equation of motion, in the same field map, integrated by Butcher's seven-stage Runge-Kutta method of order six at 0.1 mm, written RK6 throughout. It has no material effects in it, so it is what the networks were trained towards, but it is not the particle's true trajectory; how far it sits from the simulated truth is the companion page <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3e35d544b9d9818282fff3be2d394009"/>. As a scale, a particle's real fibre-tracker state differs from the reference by a median of 1,896 µm radially, ten times the errors below.
# 2. Aims
The question, as written in the folder README before anything was built:
> For a fixed step $`\Delta z = L/N`$ with $`N`$ in 2, 64, 128 and 256 and $`q`$ in 2, 4, 8 and 16 Gauss-Legendre stages: how accurately does **one** network, applied $`N`$ times, carry a track from the last UT plane to the first SciFi plane? Where along the crossing does its error arise? How does it compare with the exact collocation scheme chained the same way, with the straight line, and with the one-network-per-step chains of the previous study?
Four rules were fixed before the runs. **Finished** means the validation error has stopped falling, by the plateau rule of protocol 8, written before any of the work that followed so that it could not be tuned to a result. The **comparators** are the exact collocation scheme chained at the same step and stage count, the ceiling; the straight line, the null; the previous study's per-step chains; and the real fibre-tracker state, the material floor. **Reported** are the endpoint error overall and per component and momentum band, the single-step error, the growth along the crossing, what the error is made of, and the cost per track. The **measure**, settled by George on 18 September 2026, is the radial distance at $`z_1`$ between the network's endpoint and the reference endpoint, $`r = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}`$, median over the 1,452 test tracks, with every comparator measured the same way.
# 3. Conclusion
- **One network, applied to itself, crosses the magnet.** Trained on nothing but the equation of motion and the collocation equations of its own step, one two-hidden-layer network of 128 units, applied 64 to 256 times, reaches a median radial error of 119 to 164 µm, against 450,542 µm for the straight line. Step and stage count stop mattering above 64 steps, and the exact scheme there is 300 to 1,000 times more accurate, so what is measured is the network and its optimiser.
- **The step is not the problem; the chain is.** One application is accurate to 0.085 to 0.81 µm, 0.4 to 2 per cent of the straight line over that step. The endpoint is 240 to 2,300 times the single step, not $`N`$ times, and the error grows by about 2.9 from half way to the end, where independent steps would give 1.41.
- **The error is early slope error carried to the end.** Summing each step's slope error multiplied by the distance it still has to travel reproduces the endpoint error to 98 to 99 per cent once both slopes are in; the position errors account for about a tenth. The increments are three times more aligned along a track than chance, but the median signed increment is 2 per cent of the median size: a correlation, not a bend.
- **The loss is spent on the wrong tracks.** Dividing each residual by the pooled spread of its component puts 96.9 per cent of the objective on 2 to 5 GeV, which holds 500 of the 1,452 test tracks, and 0.6 per cent on 10 to 50 GeV; and it weights the last quarter of the crossing, where an error costs least, as heavily as the first.
- **Convergence must be read from the validation error, never from the loss.** The optimiser goes on cutting the rescaled objective by more than 1 per cent per restart long after the endpoint has stopped improving; and the loss was flat for ten rounds while the validation error was still falling 5 to 24 per cent per ten rounds in eleven runs of eleven. Training was stopped once on the loss alone; extending bought a further 12 to 42 per cent.
- **Caveats.** One seed per network, and a run's ten-round validation spread is 9 to 21 per cent at 64 steps and above, so smaller differences between cells are not measurements. Six of the sixteen were still drifting down when the farm was emptied, so the headline is "best after 1,000 to 1,758 restarts", not a strict plateau.
- **Next steps, as they happened.** Reweight the loss (built and measured, results with George, no write-up yet, so link it here when it exists); move the momentum window down to about 5 GeV, which George's supervisors asked for on 21 September 2026; settle the reference, now its own study; and take on the $`y`$ plane, the larger part of what the reweighted networks get wrong.
